Chapter 2

The value illusion and the primitive brain

The value illusion and the primitive brain

Set your phone down on the desk, or take your hand off your mouse, and dare your craving to click the next chapter by itself. Without your cooperation, an urge cannot lift a finger or press a button because it has no muscles of its own. You are the one holding the screen.

When you end up four hours past bedtime after promising yourself you would stop at eleven, two separate systems are running on top of each other inside your head. For years, they have sounded like one voice.

Most quit guides, including the original EasyPeasy hackbook and several recovery programs, call urges a “Little Monster” or an inner “Beast.” That framing makes the mechanics easy to picture, yet it turns your own mind into a battleground. Treating a normal part of your biology like an invading enemy turns every late-night urge into a fight against yourself, draining your energy and feeding guilt.

Nothing evil lives inside your skull. A late-night binge comes from two ordinary parts working together: your primitive brain, older biological wiring operating in an environment it was never built for, and the value illusion, a stack of learned beliefs telling you that obeying those impulses is worth the cost.

System 1: The primitive brain

Built into the older part of your brain is a basic reward circuit, which we will call the primitive brain. This wiring evolved over millions of years to keep humans alive. Its job is straightforward: notice anything new, seek out food and social warmth, and keep you alert until an unresolved threat or problem is settled. Back when snapping branches in the dark meant real danger, staying tense until you knew what moved kept you alive.

The catch is that this older system is illiterate and literal. It cannot tell the difference between mastering a tough skill in real life and watching a “Level Up” window flash across a web novel screen. To that wiring, sixty hours of voiced banter in a visual novel feels close enough to real companionship, and rushing through forty chapters of Vinland Saga at two in the morning satisfies the urge for closure just as well as reading it calmly during the day.

In physical terms, that impulse is mild. Unlike withdrawing from alcohol or heavy stimulants, stopping mid-chapter brings no fever or shaking. It feels closer to a faint itch: restless fingers and mild mental static over an unfinished scene. Left alone for a minute or two, that sensation fades on its own.

So how can a mild itch keep smart people glued to a phone until dawn? Because the primitive brain cannot move your thumb, it hijacks your inner voice using the word “I.” Right as a cliffhanger drops or a difficult work task stalls, a calm thought slips into your head: “I just want to see who wins this fight. I am not sleepy yet anyway, and one more episode will help me settle down.” Because that thought says “I,” you assume it is your own preference, and you start debating yourself as if two people were arguing in your living room.

To expose the impersonation in real time, answer that thought with a flat, final no and put the device down. Half a second later, listen to how the wording shifts: “Come on, you are wide awake anyway. How are we supposed to go to sleep right in the middle of that?” The “I” flips instantly into “you” and “we.” Seeing that pronoun flip removes any reason to fight or hate yourself; you are simply watching an automatic urge ask for quick closure while pretending to be your own decision.

That older wiring runs into trouble because modern fiction is packaged to never let you finish. A standalone book or movie ends, and you put it away. Today, algorithms stand ready to queue up fourteen hundred chapters of “Reincarnated With an S-Rank Cheat Skill” the second you finish one story. Across manga, anime, and novels, the delivery format strips away every natural stopping point.

Web novels break stories into daily bite-sized installments ending right in the middle of a fight or a secret, paired with rapid stat jumps and arrogant rivals getting humiliated on schedule. Instead of a back cover, every page ends on a Next Chapter arrow. Manga works the same nerve visually through page-turn reveals, dramatic spreads, and weekly cliffhangers, while pirate aggregator sites remove the physical pause of finishing a volume and keep you scrolling straight into fifty similar titles.

Anime packs that tension into tight twenty-four-minute episodes timed to peak right before the credits roll, then throws a five-second Next Episode countdown over the screen before your rational mind even gets a vote. Add loud action scenes, cliffhanger episode endings, and ten seasonal shows airing every week, and there is always another episode waiting. Light novels stretch the same rhythm across twenty or thirty volumes, mixing easy wish-fulfillment fantasies with character art that makes daydreaming effortless.

Visual novels pull off the sneakiest trick of all by selling the illusion of personal agency. Branching dialogue choices, affection meters, unlockable CG galleries, and dozens of hours of intimate voice acting convince your primitive brain that you are building real relationships and calling the shots, turning passive screen time into a completionist chore.

Even great series like Slam Dunk or Vinland Saga trip this same wiring because good storytelling creates real emotional tension. When a tight sports match hits its final minutes or a character faces a brutal turning point, your primitive brain does not pause to admire the craft. All it registers past midnight is an unresolved situation, pushing you to keep turning pages even when you are too exhausted to appreciate what you are looking at.

System 2: The value illusion

If the primitive brain only produces a brief restless itch that fades within minutes when ignored, why do you end up pinned to a screen for four hours night after night? Standing behind that tiny itch is the value illusion, which EasyPeasy calls brainwashing. The value illusion is the stack of comfortable excuses you have absorbed from past nights, online fandoms, and friend groups. It tells you that bingeing relieves workday stress, that quiet evenings would feel unbearable without a feed to scroll, that staying up until dawn finishing an acclaimed anime proves you appreciate art, or that abandoning a five-hundred-chapter web novel wastes all the hours you already spent. Often it adds one easy comparison: at least you are reading stories instead of doomscrolling short videos.

That split explains why every past attempt built on willpower left you drained. Trying to hold yourself back while you still believe the value illusion puts two halves of your mind at odds, forcing you to refuse something you still view as a genuine comfort and reward. As long as abstaining feels like punishment, a mild restless urge from your primitive brain triggers an overwhelming sense of missing out. Breaking after a few days has nothing to do with weak willpower; people naturally go back to things they still believe are good for them.

Flipping that order solves the struggle. When you dismantle every piece of the value illusion first, you see plainly that the binge loop gives you neither real rest nor a better experience of stories.

Once those illusions collapse, the primitive brain loses its best arguments, though its old habit loops will not vanish overnight. Even after you see through every excuse, that automated wiring can still throw out loud “I” thoughts and sudden waves of restlessness, especially when you are exhausted or stressed. Even when those sudden thoughts feel loud and urgent, recognizing them as an old habit signal rather than a real decision removes their hold over you.

Once you separate those two layers, the urge stops looking like a mysterious flaw in your personality. There is just an automatic urge asking for quick closure, backed up by habits and excuses you picked up over the years. Separating the itch from the excuse means you stop fighting your own biology, and once those excuses fall apart, the impulse loses its hold over your evenings.
