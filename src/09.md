# Chapter 9: The "good art" alibi

Most readers admit fairly quickly that copy-paste web novels are junk. Where the value illusion holds on tightest is with stories you genuinely respect. When you stay up until three in the morning reading *Slam Dunk* or *Vinland Saga*, watching an acclaimed anime, or finishing a brilliant visual novel route, the habit wears a respectable disguise. You do not hide it out of embarrassment like cheap filler. In fact, many fans talk about finishing a series in one sleepless weekend as proof of good taste and proof of how great the story was.

High artistic quality deserves respect, yet staying up until dawn to rush through a classic has very little to do with appreciating it. Good writing and sharp artwork never shut off the binge switch. Worse, once fatigue sets in past midnight, a marathon takes away the exact qualities that made a masterpiece worth picking up in the first place.

Strong authors make you care about what happens next. When Shohoku falls behind by four points in the final minutes against Sannoh, or when Thorfinn faces a painful turning point in *Vinland Saga*, the emotional tension feels real. Your primitive brain, however, cannot tell Takehiko Inoue's drawing style apart from a cheap cultivation cliffhanger. All it senses past midnight is an unresolved fight or an open mystery, and all it wants is the answer right now.

Watch what happens to your reading pace once that rush takes over at two in the morning. Inoue spent years putting deliberate silence into *Slam Dunk*: a tired player catching his breath, a close-up of nervous eyes, a brief look between teammates right before the last shot. Makoto Yukimura built entire chapters of *Vinland Saga* around slow farm conversations meant to take time and sink in. When you speed through four volumes half-asleep just to see who wins the match or survives the duel, you barely look at a single drawing for more than a second. Those careful pauses turn into obstacles you skip past just to see the final score.

The same blur happens when you stack six heavy anime episodes in one night, push through two dense light novel volumes before sunrise, or click through an eight-hour stretch of a top visual novel. A tired mind can only absorb so much emotional weight before scenes start running together. Big twists lose half their impact because you never gave the previous reveal ten minutes to settle. You tune out the music, miss small hints in the dialogue, and wake up the next morning remembering only a bare plot outline while forgetting why the story felt moving at all. You can tell yourself you were admiring great art, yet in practice you treated a masterpiece like a quick summary just to get to the end.

Part of this mistake comes from a common compliment in fandom: people say a story was so good they literally could not put it down, as if losing sleep proves how much you loved it. Think back, though, to the stories that still stay clear in your memory years later versus the ones you rushed through in one sleepless weekend. The scenes that stick with you are almost always the ones you had enough energy to sit with and think about afterward. Racing through a great series while half-asleep turns an unforgettable classic into a blurry memory you have to reread later just to recall what happened.

Even after noticing how marathon reading dulls the story, the value illusion usually tries two practical excuses to keep the book open past bedtime. First it warns that stopping right before a climax ruins the momentum and breaks the mood. Next it tells you that even if you close the series now, you will lie awake staring at the ceiling thinking about the next chapter anyway, so you might as well finish the arc tonight.

Neither threat holds up once you test it. Good suspense does not spoil just because you went to sleep for eight hours. When you pause *Slam Dunk* or shut off an intense anime episode at eleven o'clock, the excitement stays intact until tomorrow, and your mind gets time overnight to replay what happened. Coming back the next afternoon with fresh focus makes the climax land much harder than pushing through chapter thirty when your eyes are sore and your brain feels heavy. Tiredness is what breaks the spell, turning real excitement into dull impatience to reach the last page.

As for lying awake thinking about what happens next, that restless feeling is simpler than it looks. Once the phone or book stays out of reach, normal curiosity usually fades after ten or fifteen minutes. Your primitive brain is simply reacting to an unfinished scene. Treating fifteen minutes of curiosity like real insomnia costs you four hours of sleep and ruins tomorrow's focus, and worse, you end up reaching the exact climax you stayed up for when you are too exhausted to enjoy it.

Great manga, anime, light novels, and visual novels deserve better than tired eyes at two in the morning. Respecting a well-made story means giving it your clear attention when you are awake enough to take it in, and closing the cover calmly before fatigue hands control back to your primitive brain.
