# Why stopping feels impossible, even when you hate it

What kind of hobby makes people wish they had the discipline to stop while they are doing it, yet leaves them twitching to go back the moment it is taken away?

On an ordinary night when nobody restricts your screen time, you rarely treat those web novel chapters or seasonal anime episodes like anything special. You skim paragraphs, let autoplay roll while half-asleep, or hold fast-forward through filler dialogue in a visual novel just to reach the next scene, half-annoyed that the story has stalled again.

Yet the instant you forbid yourself for a single evening, say by swearing off your reader app on a Sunday night, that exact mediocre filler suddenly looks priceless. Ten minutes after turning off the screen, an odd restlessness sets in, and going to sleep without one quick check starts feeling hard to stand. How can something you barely enjoyed while doing it feel so hard to give up when you try to stop?

## The backwards loop

Things you genuinely enjoy work in one direction. After a good dinner with friends or two hours watching an unforgettable film in a theater, you walk out satisfied and move on with your week. You do not sit at your desk at three o'clock on a Tuesday twitching for another film right that second just to feel normal.

Compulsive reading and watching runs backwards because two separate discomforts stack on top of each other. Real life brings stress, boredom, and loneliness, which call for rest, focus, or real company. When you binge late at night, though, your primitive brain piles a second layer onto those normal feelings: unresolved cliffhangers hanging in your memory from last night, along with a slight dip in baseline dopamine after hours of fast stimulation.

That leftover agitation drives the eleven o'clock reach. You open a light novel or anime site because ignoring it leaves a nagging itch from unfinished scenes. When you tap Next Chapter or hit play, the tension from last night's hook eases off and your brain gets a quick boost. Turning down yesterday's noise feels relieving enough that your mind hands all the credit to the app, forgetting that yesterday's binge planted the hook and drained you in the first place. Meanwhile, the real tiredness and loneliness sit untouched underneath, usually worse the next morning because you lost another chunk of sleep.

When a noisy fan shuts off in a quiet room, you feel immediate relief, yet the fan never added peace to your afternoon; it only stopped annoying you. Screen compulsion works the same way, because people who have not wired their evenings around endless feeds and cliffhangers do not lie in bed at midnight aching for a stat screen or an extra visual novel route. Only the person caught in the loop has to keep clicking just to reach the baseline calm everyone else gets for free.

Fear is what keeps smart readers trapped even when they hate half of what they consume. The value illusion convinces you that stepping away from endless feeds leaves your evenings dull and restless forever, as if once your brain learns to crave rapid story hits you are stuck craving them for life. When you pull away, that urgent longing turns out to be mostly fear of sitting through a few quiet minutes while an unresolved hook fades out on its own.

## The time cost

Whenever the value illusion insists your late-night routine is harmless, two blunt questions settle the argument. First, think back to the day before you downloaded your first web novel reader, bookmarked a manga aggregator, or pulled an all-nighter on an anime or visual novel. Knowing how it all turns out, would you step back into that loop on purpose? Nobody ever says yes. Second, if a younger sibling or your own child stayed up until three in the morning skimming machine-translated cultivation chapters instead of sleeping, would you pat them on the back and call it a healthy hobby?

Nobody wants someone they love stuck inside this routine because everyone already senses what it costs. You can only call a habit harmless as long as you avoid adding up the hours.

Even at a conservative estimate of two and a half hours a day, the numbers add up fast. That averages the nights when you stay up three or four hours past bedtime with smaller daytime habits, like checking new chapter drops at work, watching an episode over lunch, and burning half of Saturday afternoon catching up on a season. Plenty of readers spend twice that, yet even two and a half hours a day adds up to seventeen and a half hours a week, or more than nine hundred hours a year. Divided into standard eight-hour shifts, that comes to roughly 114 full workdays. At about twenty-two working days a month, you are handing over more than five months of full-time hours every single year.

No sensible person would turn down five extra months of paid leave every twelve months. Think about what five open months look like when you keep them: sleeping through the night and waking up rested, enjoying weekends without feeling guilty, spending relaxed evenings with friends, and having open time for hobbies or projects whenever you want. Yet as long as the value illusion runs your evenings, you trade away those five months every year for half-remembered filler arcs and side characters you will forget by next Tuesday.

Once those numbers sit in front of you, the real question stands out. If the trade is that lopsided, why would a smart person keep paying the bill night after night? Because the value illusion hides the real cost behind comforting excuses that sound sensible when you are tired.
