# Chapter 8: Deserting Reality: The Seduction of Flat Dimensions

Let's sit down, you and I, for a conversation instead of a lecture. I want to ask about the thing the Big Monster whispers most often and most convincingly. It makes you reach for the phone at 9:47 PM and not put it down until 2:34 AM, makes Tuesday afternoon feel like a gray, endless corridor, and makes the fantasy world feel like the only place you're alive.

The Big Monster says: *"Real life is too boring, too mundane, too demanding. Reality can't compete. In the novel I'm powerful and loved and the world is vivid and vast. Here I'm ordinary, and I can't bear ordinary."*

I hear the grief in that voice, and the hunger under it: for a life that feels alive, a self that feels powerful, a world that feels meaningful. The hunger is real and legitimate, and I'm not here to take it from you.

I do want to ask a question, and I'd like you to answer it for yourself and not for me. Is reality actually flat, or has something been dimming it?

## The photograph and the meal

I can hear the objection rising before I finish the question: *You don't know my life, my job, my commute, my exhaustion, my small, repetitive, grinding days. You don't know what it's like to sit in this body on a Tuesday afternoon and feel nothing, no excitement, no meaning, no spark, just the flat gray hum of existing. The fantasy world is the only place I feel like I matter.*

I believe you. The Tuesday afternoon feels flat, the commute feels gray, and the exhaustion is real. I won't answer with "life is beautiful if you just look," because your grief deserves a hearing before it can be examined.

So here is a question to sit with before you answer: when did it start feeling this way? Was Tuesday afternoon always this gray and the commute always this empty, or did the flatness arrive at some point? If it did, what arrived with it? When did the reading habit begin, when did the four-hour sessions start, when did 2:34 AM become routine, and when did Tuesday start feeling like a corridor? Did the flatness come before the reading or after it? Did the reading cure the flatness, or create it?

I won't answer that for you, but think back to the sunglasses from last chapter. If you put on dark glasses and walked outside and the world looked dim, you'd say the glasses were dim. If you wore them for months and forgot you had them on, you might conclude the world had always been dim, until you felt the weight on your nose and wondered what was on your face. The sunglasses are the Parasite. It dims the world and then says, "See? The world is dim." The dimness is real, but it belongs to the glasses, and the glasses can come off.

Now look at what the fantasy actually gives you, in the body, in the hours, and in the morning after. It gives you a brief golden flicker when the protagonist levels up, a borrowed, vicarious hit that fades in seconds, so you need another. The flickers get thinner and the scrolling gets faster. Meanwhile you're horizontal and motionless, with burning eyes, a locked neck, shallow breathing, a foggy brain, and a body that hasn't moved in four hours. You call that being alive, powerful, the protagonist. But you're the reader, in a bed in the dark, and the protagonist's victory isn't yours.

Now look at the "small, gray, limited" reality. It gives you the actual taste of food, warm and complex and specific, in a meal you cooked or someone made for you. It gives you your feet on the ground, with real weight and pressure and texture. It gives you a friend's voice, a stranger's laugh, a bird outside the window. It gives you the warmth and tiredness of your muscles after a walk, a vast, unrepeatable sky changing color (nothing like a panel), and the feeling of your own hands building, cooking, fixing, or making something. That feeling is slow and warm. It settles in the body and stays, with no screen needed and no burning eyes afterward. It's yours, neither borrowed nor simulated.

Suppose I offered you two meals. One is a glossy, high-resolution photograph of a meal that you can zoom in on and scroll past but can't taste, smell, or feel warm in your hands. The other is simple: rice, vegetables, a piece of fish, cooked by someone who cared. You sit down, eat, taste the warmth, and feel the fullness stay. Which one is real, which one is the Parasite's sugar water, and which one are you hungry for?

## The six-inch sky

The next objection comes after that comparison: *I've tried being present. I've tried enjoying small things and finding meaning in the ordinary, and it feels forced and empty. The ordinary is just ordinary, and it isn't enough. I need the extraordinary, and the extraordinary is in the fantasy.*

I hear the pain of trying and failing, of reaching for the walk, the meal, the Tuesday afternoon and feeling nothing. I'd ask why it didn't work, and in particular what state your body was in when you tried. Were you rested and clear, with fresh eyes, a sharp mind, and deep breathing? Or were you depleted and foggy, with eyes dry from four hours of blue-white light, a locked neck, shallow breathing, and a mind buzzing with the residue of the Post-Consumption Hangover?

If you spent four hours in front of a bright screen and then looked at a sunset that seemed dim, you wouldn't conclude the sunset was dim. You'd conclude your eyes were tired. If you spent four hours in a loud room and then walked into a quiet garden that felt empty, you'd know your ears were ringing. If you ate a beautiful meal after four hours of chewing ice, your tongue would be numb and the meal would taste bland, but the numbness would belong to the ice. The screen, the noise, and the ice all stand for the reading. The Parasite tires your eyes, rings your ears, and numbs your tongue, and then says: *"See? Reality isn't enough."* The dimness, the emptiness, and the blandness are real, but they aren't the sunset's, the garden's, or the meal's. When the Parasite is removed, the world will be the world, and it will be enough.

Now look at the fantasy with your own eyes. What is it made of? Words on a screen, panels in a grid, pixels in a rectangle, a six-inch glowing square six inches from your face in a dark room while your body lies still. The vivid world is a sequence of text blocks you skim, the vast landscape is a paragraph you skipped, the epic battle is a stat window, and the deep romance is a blush and a stammer. That boundless, free world is a six-inch rectangle.

You're in a bed in a room in a building in a city under a sky. The sky is real and vast, and it isn't six inches wide. It needs no battery and doesn't end on a cliffhanger.

And reality, the supposedly small and limited one, is made of actual light, the kind that warms your skin, changes color at six, seven, and eight in the evening, and moves shadows across the floor. It's made of actual sound: a bird, rain, a friend's voice, your own breathing. It has texture in the roughness of bark, the smoothness of a stone, the warmth of a cup, and the weight of a book. It has taste and smell, salt and sweetness and bitterness, rain on hot pavement, bread in the oven, grass after mowing. It has your body, with its weight and warmth, your feet on the ground, your lungs filling, and your heart beating. None of that is a flicker. It's a warmth that doesn't fade in seconds or need a screen.

So which dimension is flat? The six-inch rectangle or the sky, the stat window or the sunset, the copy-paste dungeon or the singular, unrepeatable Tuesday afternoon you're sitting in right now?

Say you sat in a dark room staring at a photograph of a forest while someone opened the window and the actual forest stood outside, with real trees, birds, wind, and the smell of earth and leaf and rain. If you looked back at the photograph and said, "The photograph is more vivid," you'd be dimmed, with the Parasite on your face. The photograph is ink on paper, and the forest is alive in three dimensions, with smell, sound, texture, wind, light, shadow, insects, earth, and sky. It needs no battery, it's outside the window, and the window is open.

## The garden has been waiting

One question is still sitting in your chest, the one you're afraid to say aloud: *I'm afraid that if I stop reading, reality will still be flat. I'm afraid the sunglasses will come off and the world will still be gray, and I'll be stuck in the ordinary forever, and the ordinary will be unbearable.*

The fear is real and understandable, and it's the Parasite's last, most tender defense. It has dimmed the world for so long that you've forgotten what the world looks like undimmed, and that forgetting feels like a loss, and the loss feels like a death. So you cling to the sunglasses because they're familiar, even though they're what's doing the harm.

I'll say this the way a friend who has already walked the path would say it, looking back: *I know. I was afraid too. Then I looked, and the world wasn't gray. It was the world, and it was enough.*

Reality won't become fantasy. Tuesday afternoon won't become a dungeon raid, the commute won't become a cultivation breakthrough, and the ordinary won't become the extraordinary. I won't promise that, because that promise is the Parasite's, and it's a lie. Reality will become reality, which is what you've been hungering for all along. Your hunger was for the real, not the fantasy, and the real isn't boring. Its vividness lives in sunlight, not in the stat window, and sunlight needs no screen, doesn't end on a cliffhanger, and doesn't leave your eyes burning.

You're afraid of the ordinary because the Parasite has told you it's unbearable and gray. But the ordinary is the texture of a life: the taste of the morning coffee, the sound of rain, your feet on the ground, the weight of your body in the chair, the breath. The breath isn't boring. It's the most alive thing you've ever felt, and you've forgotten it because the Parasite filled the silence it lived in.

The garden from the last chapter is still alive under the vine. It isn't demanding or shouting that it's more vivid than the fantasy. It's just being alive, patient, warm, and real, and it doesn't need you to believe in it. It needs you to look, and looking is the sunglasses coming off. The garden doesn't blame you for the vine, and it waits. You're already looking, because you're reading this, and the reading is the beginning of its return.

That garden includes your real love: *Vagabond*, *Slam Dunk*, the physical book on the Sunday afternoon table that you savor and close with a satisfied sigh. It includes the bounded, completed, nourishing masterwork that gives you something and lets you go. The vine is the infinite, serialized junk, the four-hundred-chapter cultivation clone and the treadmill, and you never needed it to love the garden.

You don't need to open a window tonight, put down the phone, or walk outside to prove the sky is real. The Paradoxical Instruction holds, and you're still reading your chapters and observing. But tonight, when the Big Monster whispers *"Real life is too boring. Fantasy is where I feel alive,"* add one small question: *Is the Parasite dimming the world? Is the flatness the sunglasses, and is the sky outside the window, waiting?* Plant it, let it sit, and let the seeing do the work.

The fantasy is the flat dimension. It's six inches wide, a stat window, a copy-paste dungeon, a blush and a stammer. Reality is the sky, which doesn't run on a battery or end on a cliffhanger. You've been looking at a six-inch rectangle and calling it the world while the world waited outside the window. The flatness you feel isn't the world's. It belongs to the Parasite's sunglasses, and when they come off the world will be warm, and the warmth will be yours. You don't need the fantasy to feel alive.

The fifth room is walked, the sunglasses are seen, and the sky is remembered. You're still reading your chapters and the observation is working. You felt it, didn't you? The aching recognition: *The flatness wasn't the world. It was the thing on my face.*

Let it sit. Tomorrow the Big Monster will offer its most comfortable, reasonable, and intellectually seductive lie: *"At least I'm not hurting anyone. At least it's not drugs or gambling. I'm just reading. It's harmless, even intellectual, and better than the alternatives."* Hear it as the Parasite's last elegant defense, the lie that says the wound is small because the blade is thin and the theft is minor because the thief is quiet.

In the next chapter I'll show you the size of the theft, with no guilt or fear, only simple arithmetic: the hours, the days, the years, what they held, and what they could have held. You may find that the harmless habit has cost you more than any needle, bottle, or casino, because the cost is measured in life and not money, and the life is yours.
